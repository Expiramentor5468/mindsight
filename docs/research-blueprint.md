# Mindsight – research and training blueprint

Research prepared September 24, 2026. This blueprint informed the MindSight rebuild. Proposed extensions are distinguished from the implemented release in the repository README.

## Recommendation

Build a voice-led practice companion around a repeatable cycle: settle, familiarize, explore an unknown target, commit, receive feedback, revisit the target, and reflect. Offer separate measurement blocks with consistent rules. Keep all exercises independently accessible.

There is enough public instructional material to create a substantive experimental program. There is not enough reliable comparative evidence to identify an optimal method, promise extraocular perception, or attach a credible success probability. The aim is to give the practice a fair, sustained trial while finding out what actually happens.

This is an original synthesis, not McNamara’s complete curriculum or a licensed version of another school’s method. All durations, trial counts, progression rules, scripts, and software requirements below are our proposed design unless expressly attributed.

## Findings that change the design

### 1. McNamara provides an actual solo-learning mechanism

In his public lesson, McNamara makes color and shape cards identifiable by touch using dried glue. The learner answers before touching the identifying mark. He then suggests staying with the card after feedback to explore how the now-known target feels. He explicitly says the method is not guaranteed. [1]

For the app, spoken feedback replaces the tactile answer key. A “stay with this” command preserves the displayed target after feedback; “next” selects a fresh one. Known-target exploration is logged separately from unknown-target accuracy.

His official companion site describes an adult, solo pilot program involving colors, shapes, black/white, letters, numbers, and journaling, potentially extending one to three years. It does not establish that this length is necessary or effective. [2]

### 2. A coach carries the organizational burden

Freeman’s session 88 explains the difficulty of being both trainee and session organizer. It includes a body relaxation, familiarization with known colors, imagery, an attention shift toward the participant’s dog, and subsequent unknown-color work. [3]

The useful software inference is to handle instructions, pacing, target selection, and notes automatically. We need not repeat the lesson’s claims about switching cerebral hemispheres. Session 97’s description states that incorrect attempts were edited out; its visible outcomes cannot estimate a success rate. [4]

Gallant’s introductory conversation favors eyes closed under the mask and acknowledges distracting light near the nose. It emphasizes relaxed, playful practice and partner support. It also suggests lengthy mask familiarization; that duration is not adopted here. [5]

### 3. Comparison can precede identification

Burgoin’s lesson progresses through light/dark, colored illumination, translucent material, illuminated opaque cards, and changes in distance. She uses comparison when someone cannot name a color and encourages reporting components of shapes. She recommends apps as one solo option. [6]

However, the lesson also discourages interpreting apparent light as leakage and includes strobe illumination. Those procedures cannot simply become evidence of nonvisual perception. Our adaptation uses steady targets, comfortable light-blocking, and recorded conditions. We borrow graduated comparison as an instructional idea, without asserting her physiological explanation or reproducing her complete method.

### 4. Preparation should accommodate different experiences

Intuitu discusses intuition, visualization, beliefs, and physical/emotional state. Its public visualization exercise asks someone to imagine a word and notice the image’s appearance and apparent location. Its free introduction is advertised as five days with one practical exercise each day. [7–9]

Our adaptation offers brief imagery exploration without making imagery compulsory. A person can record a word, impression, sensation, or no impression. Failure to identify targets is never described as insufficient belief, a blocked ability, or the learner’s fault.

### 5. Intensive marketing does not identify the fastest method

Vibravision advertises immersions combining breathwork, movement, meditation, visualization, and perception activities. The reviewed public pages do not provide a complete reproducible curriculum or a controlled comparison showing accelerated acquisition. [10]

MatrixVision publishes seven stage labels and a 40-day integration format, while acknowledging that the method is not scientifically proven and outcomes vary, including no result. Its detailed lessons were not accessed. [11]

We can adopt short, well-organized practice blocks and continuity across days. We cannot responsibly equate five days of attendance with five days to a demonstrated ability.

### 6. Measurement protects the value of the practice

Gómez-Marín’s 2023 document is a research proposal containing preliminary observations, not a completed validation trial. It discusses masks, barriers, randomized digital targets, and keeping the target unknown to the experimenter. [12]

INAB reports testing a sensor-equipped blindfold in 67 adults: residual light could be measured even when people reported darkness, and fit affected leakage. That supports measuring and recording conditions; it does not establish whether any particular demonstration used that leakage. [13]

Intuitu’s own research discussion describes a participant identifying physical imperfections in colored cups as a possible unintended cue. It explicitly says preliminary results do not by themselves demonstrate extraocular vision. [14]

Grinberg’s historical article describes training 19 children using breathing, meditation, trainer interaction, and blindfold tasks. This is historical background, not a sufficient basis for an adult self-guided treatment or an effectiveness claim. Its forced-breathing procedure is excluded from this design. [15]

## The proposed program

### Session structure: roughly 20–25 minutes, adjustable

The default is a practical starting dose, not an evidence-based prescription. Shorten it whenever tired, uncomfortable, or frustrated. Four sessions a week for an initial four-week evaluation is a manageable proposal, not a required commitment.

| Segment | Default | What the user does |
|---|---:|---|
| Readiness | 2 minutes | Confirm audible output and an actual recognized spoken phrase; position screen and check mask |
| Settle | 2–3 minutes | Sit comfortably, breathe normally, release shoulder/jaw tension, let attention settle |
| Familiarize | 3 minutes | Explore announced examples from the current target set; no scoring |
| Practice | 8–10 minutes | Describe impressions, commit or pass, hear feedback, optionally stay with the target |
| Quiet measurement | 5 minutes or a short fixed trial block | Answer under fixed rules; feedback waits until the block ends |
| Reflect | 1–2 minutes | Dictate what felt useful and whether to repeat or change an element next time |

Measurement can be skipped during onboarding. Thereafter schedule it predictably, rather than only after unusually good practice. If time expires, save a partial block and complete the predeclared trial count later under the same conditions.

### Orientation session

First explain that the screen stays visible throughout each active trial. The blindfold blocks ordinary viewing. The app does not wait for an answer to draw the target.

Let the user hear a sample prompt, answer, confirmation, feedback, and “next” transition before masking. Verify commands with an actual spoken exchange. If speech recognition cannot work reliably, report that before practice starts.

Give one minute with a neutral blank screen to notice ordinary background imagery or sensations, logged as a baseline without scoring. Then demonstrate announced colors. Finish with a few unknown targets and explain the difference between an observation, a committed answer, and feedback.

### Exercise A – known-target familiarization

Choose two distinct colors, initially red and blue. Announce and display one, then let the learner describe any impression. Switch on request. Repeat a few times, without requiring a specific sensation or a vivid image.

This is an association and attention exercise. Because the answer is given, success cannot be scored. A sensation noticed after hearing “red” is not evidence of detecting an unknown red target.

The two-color choice reduces task complexity but gives a high 50% chance baseline. It must never be used to make impressive-looking claims from a handful of correct responses.

### Exercise B – unknown color with feedback

Draw red or blue independently at random; repeated colors are allowed. Display it immediately at a fixed screen position and steady brightness. Say only that the target is ready.

Accept unrestricted observations without scoring. At commitment, confirm the interpreted answer before locking it. Announce the true color after locking, save the trial, and leave the target displayed. Offer “stay,” “next,” or a note. Passing is always permitted and recorded.

After familiarization with the interaction, offer four colors – red, blue, yellow, green – with a 25% chance baseline. The first intended answer is the outcome. Later guesses can be logged as exploration but never overwrite it.

### Exercise C – same or different

Present target A, then target B on a voice command, retaining each until the user is ready to move on. Ask whether they are the same or different. Draw that relation with equal probability, then construct the targets to match it.

With four colors, independently drawing A and B would produce “different” 75% of the time. Explicitly balancing the relation avoids that trap. Announced examples are separate from unannounced trials. This is our comparison drill, inspired by contrast-first teaching, not a verified mindsight intervention.

### Exercise D – location

Use one constant shape and color. Randomize its position between two fixed regions. The learner says left or right; chance is 50%. Keep size, sound, and timing constant. Later offer four quadrants with a 25% baseline.

Stay seated. This screen exercise does not establish the ability to navigate safely while blindfolded.

### Exercise E – shape and orientation

Start with circle versus triangle, then circle, triangle, square, and star. Use the same foreground/background colors and bounding size. Describe curves, straight lines, or points before committing to a shape.

Keep the original description for reflection but score only the exact selected shape. Add horizontal versus vertical bars as a separate task; avoid changing color, location, and shape simultaneously at first.

### Exercise F – symbols and images

Offer a clearly declared small set of letters or digits before expanding the set. Ambiguous speech must be resolved before feedback. Full alphabet and digit tasks remain available for exploration, without implying readiness from one lucky block.

Photographs are an optional descriptive activity. Save notes before reveal. Do not let an AI invent an accuracy percentage by finding loose similarities afterward. Meaningful image testing would need a separate, prespecified judging procedure with decoys and blinded evaluation.

### Optional physical-card companion

Support spoken timing and journaling while the user practices with physical cards. The app cannot verify an off-screen card by itself. User-reported results must be labeled as such. Tactile feedback marks can be encountered accidentally, and differences in wear or texture can identify cards; this mode is practice, not strong validation.

### Optional remote-viewing branch

Keep it separate from displayed-target mindsight. IRVA emphasizes concealed tasking and recorded impressions before feedback. [16] A future branch can use unknown photographs and sealed notes, but it needs its own scoring design. Remote-viewing results should not be merged with color-identification statistics.

## Four-week introductory schedule

Weeks organize exposure, not promised acquisition. Exercises remain accessible at any time.

| Week | Main purpose | Practice | Decision afterward |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Learn the interface and establish a baseline | Known colors, two-color unknowns, optional same/different | Is the setup comfortable and voice control reliable? |
| 2 | Explore distinctions | Four-color practice; brief shape introduction | Which task is engaging enough to repeat consistently? |
| 3 | Compare one preparation choice | Same primary task; alternate short settling versus settling plus familiarization | Is any difference repeatable, or just a good day? |
| 4 | Repeat with fixed conditions | Keep one target set and complete predeclared measurement blocks | Continue, adjust, or stop based on experience and results |

For a preparation comparison, alternate the two conditions in a schedule chosen before collecting results. Keep the main task, lighting, mask, and scoring unchanged. Both conditions include equivalent trial counts. Small personal comparisons remain exploratory.

Do not promote difficulty automatically after a single high score. Offer it for interest, but label demonstrated improvement only cautiously, after repeated fixed-condition results and checks for cues. If performance remains near chance, say so plainly and avoid prescribing ever-increasing practice as the inevitable solution.

## Voice interaction specification

Voice is enabled by default after the initial user gesture and permission flow. The user should be able to finish a normal session without lifting the mask.

| User intent | Examples | Required behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Observe | “There’s a reddish patch”; “nothing yet” | Save as an observation; do not score |
| Commit | “My answer is red”; “I choose blue” | Read back the interpretation and request confirmation |
| Confirm/correct | “Yes”; “No, I said blue” | Lock only the confirmed answer; retain correction history |
| Get help | “What am I doing?”; “what are my options?” | Explain the current task without revealing the target |
| Manage pace | “More time”; “stay with this”; “next” | Preserve or advance the target as appropriate |
| Manage speech | “Less talking”; “repeat that” | Adjust guidance or repeat the last safe instruction |
| Interrupt | “Pause”; “stop”; “end session” | Stop promptly, save current state, announce status |
| Report a problem | “I can see around the mask”; “you misheard me” | Pause and flag the trial; never silently discard it |

Proposed short script: “A target is on screen. Notice whatever comes up. You can describe it, make an answer, or pass.” Silence follows. If the user says “nothing,” respond only if needed: “That’s okay. More time, a guess, or a pass?”

After a confirmed practice answer: “You chose blue. The target was red. Stay with it, or say next.” Avoid suggesting that a wrong answer secretly represents another target or future trial.

The conversational coach should not receive the current unknown target. A separate deterministic component manages targets, commitments, and feedback. Otherwise personalized encouragement could inadvertently expose the answer.

Do not mistake the app’s own speech for a user answer. A microphone failure pauses and announces the interruption; a listening-status query is available. If the platform cannot support dependable hands-free use, the readiness screen must say so. Browser implementation choices still require platform testing.

Autosave every commitment and interruption. Repeating a block creates a new block. Notes before and after feedback remain distinguishable. Export should preserve raw trial history and the protocol version, without requiring audio retention.

## Measurement rules

1. Declare the target set, probability distribution, number of trials, mask/eye condition, and feedback mode before a block.
2. Generate each target independently, allowing repeats. Do not cycle through a balanced deck in a way that lets the final target be inferred.
3. Keep unknown targets out of spoken labels, accessibility announcements, notifications, and coaching context. Screen-reader access still needs usable controls, but must not announce the answer before commitment.
4. Count correct, incorrect, passed, interrupted, and protocol-compromised trials. Report accuracy among answered trials alongside coverage across all planned trials; never hide passes or exclusions.
5. Use one confirmed answer per trial. A post-feedback correction cannot improve the score. Recognition errors discovered afterward are annotated separately.
6. Keep sample size and chance baseline visible in summaries. Report uncertainty, not only percentages or streaks.
7. Choose an initial fixed measurement target such as 100 four-color trials, accumulated across sessions. This is a bookkeeping choice, not a validated diagnostic threshold. Review after the full block, not whenever the score happens to look good.
8. Repeatedly checking many tasks, stopping on success, or choosing the best-looking session exaggerates apparent evidence. A promising result should lead to a new confirmation block with the same rules.
9. A high score under a mask is still not proof that ordinary sight was excluded. Record fit checks and any visible glow. Do not tighten a mask painfully or assume “it feels dark” certifies complete occlusion.
10. Stronger follow-up can use an independent opaque screen barrier while the target remains rendered. That is a separate condition and may change the claimed task; do not pool it with ordinary practice. Avoid improvised enclosures that cause discomfort or device overheating.

Flat-color displays differ in luminance even when RGB values seem similarly bright. A consumer screen cannot certify equal perceived brightness or a light-tight mask. Shape, location, and controlled-barrier tasks can help investigate alternatives, but home results remain limited.

## Elements excluded from the default

No forceful breathwork, prolonged breath holds, strobe lights, deliberately induced afterimages, uncomfortable eye movements, blows/taps to startle the learner, or blindfolded walking. No required religious imagery. No claim that binaural beats, pineal activation, a particular frequency, or left/right-brain switching is necessary or established for this outcome.

The program should support curiosity without requiring conviction. It should neither promise success nor reinterpret every miss as progress. Pleasant practice, improved attention, and accurate identification of unknown targets are distinct outcomes.

## Remaining limits and build decision

Public lessons support a real, implementable experimental curriculum; the earlier conclusion that little could be done without paid materials was too restrictive. They do not establish the optimal protocol or verify the demonstrations’ extraordinary claims.

The full McNamara book, paid MatrixVision lessons, proprietary Vibravision sequences, and subscriber-delivered Intuitu course were not reviewed. No purchases, enrollments, or messages to instructors were made. No comparative trial demonstrating a best training method was found in this search. The research proposal and historical report should not be presented as completed modern validation.

Proceed with the original program specified here: voice-led preparation, known/unknown distinction, feedback-and-revisit learning, accessible exercise selection, preserved trial records, and a separate measurement mode. Validate usability and target handling before making any claims about training outcomes.
